About

The reading

One first reading of James Joyce's Ulysses, cover to cover, from the 7th to the 17th of July 2026. Eleven days, sixteen logged sessions on Claude, then a parallel session in ChatGPT. Everything on this site descends from that reading, and from the documents built while the book was fresh.

The reader is Hulki Okan Tabak.

The edition

Penguin Modern Classics, introduction and notes by Declan Kiberd (1922 text). The reference documents cite by episode rather than page, so they survive an edition change. Joyce printed no episode titles. Telemachus, Calypso and the rest come from schemas he circulated privately and then contradicted between versions; where this project uses them, it uses them as scaffolding, and says so (anchor A2).

There is no read-aloud button

Your browser and your computer already have one, and it is better than anything we would build. It uses your voice, at your speed, with your pronunciation, and it tells nobody what you read. Ours would offer one voice, no speed, and no way to move by heading.

There is a harder reason. To sound warm, most machines would have to send this page's text away to be spoken on someone else's computer. On the Reading Log that text would be your own entries, from the page that promises they never leave your device. So that page carries no voice, and nothing that could make one.

What we owe the voice you already have is a page it can say correctly. The French and the Latin are marked as French and Latin. Where the transcription marks italics with underscores, the page sets them as italics rather than reading the underscores at you.

Decided 25 July 2026 by the build panel, at the author's request for a warm voice — panel session 06. It is a decision, not a fact, and it can be re-opened.

Rights note — read before publishing anything

The site is public. The repository is private, but everything that reaches the site is published. Three lines govern what may go up.

1. Ulysses (1922) is in the public domain. Quotation from the novel is unrestricted. Quote it freely and at length.

2. The apparatus around it is not. Declan Kiberd's introduction and notes in the Penguin Modern Classics edition are in copyright, as are Gifford's Ulysses Annotated, the Slote/Mamigonian/Turner Annotations, and every other secondary work this project cites. Paraphrase them in our own words and cite the source. Do not reproduce their text, and do not reconstruct a source's argument point by point in a way that substitutes for reading it.

3. Dimension 16's sources are reviews of a 2026 film. The film, its stills and its script are in copyright, and so is every review the comparison rests on. Paraphrase and cite; publish no stills without a licence; quote no review at length.

4. Emily Wilson's 2017 translation is in copyright (W.W. Norton). Dimension 16 compares it directly, which needs short parallel extracts — ordinary critical practice. Keep extracts brief, always name Wilson and the translation, and publish no continuous run of it. The same holds for any other translation the project quotes.

5. Commentary generated by other models is not our own writing. The documents in library/ were generated by a third model and supplied by the author. They are held as a cited source, never republished wholesale, and anything drawn from them is attributed to that source and marked for what it is — sourced fact, second-hand report, or the commentator's interpretation (anchor A5, catch C-23).

6. The PDF register embeds its typefaces, so the typefaces had to be free to embed. A downloadable PDF carries a subset of every font it renders, which makes typography a rights question rather than a taste question — the system serif this site uses on screen is a commercially licensed face, and embedding it in a public download is a licence question the project does not need to have. The PDFs are therefore set in DejaVu Serif and DejaVu Sans Mono, under the Bitstream Vera / DejaVu licence, which grants the right to reproduce and distribute the fonts — embedding included — on one condition: the copyright and permission notice travels with them. It does: design/fonts/LICENSE_DEJAVU.txt in the repository, and a line in every PDF's colophon. The fonts are build-time only and are never served by the site.

7. Dimension 14, the Atlas, is built from freely licensed material and nothing else. Rescoped by the author on 19 July 2026: publicly available sources only, and if a rights problem could not be avoided the dimension was to be struck. It can be avoided, so these are the rules it is built under (catch C-32).

  • Every image carries a named licence at a named URL on the credits page. No image enters the Atlas without one. "Probably old enough" is not a licence.

  • An institution's terms are not the copyright. The National Library of Ireland and Tailte Éireann both assert re-use conditions over their own scans while holding no copyright in the underlying works. Source the same material from a repository that imposes no such terms — Wikimedia Commons, per file. Do not source from nli.ie or its Flickr stream, whose terms are private-research-only.

  • Attribution lives on the credits page, never on a panel. CC BY 4.0 §3(a)(2) expressly permits this, and Creative Commons' own guidance names the case. There is a trap attached: CC BY-SA 2.0 requires credit at least as prominent as any other authorship credit that appears, so the colophon is lawful only while no panel carries any credit at all. Warburg's refusal of the caption and the licence compliance are one decision. Adding a single signature or caption to a panel would breach every share-alike licence on it simultaneously.

  • Do not crop share-alike images to fit the grid — pad them uncropped, which keeps the panels clear of the adaptation boundary. Licence the panels themselves CC BY-SA 4.0.

  • A watermark is a credit on the panel, so a watermarked file cannot be used. Added 25 July 2026, from the place-layer survey, which found a correctly licensed CC BY 2.0 photograph of the Martello tower carrying "Alessandro Ambrosetti | 2015" burned into the pixels. The no-caption rule above is what makes the colophon lawful, and it is satisfied vacuously only while no panel carries any credit at all — a credit in the image is still a credit, whoever put it there. The escape that looks obvious is closed: removing a watermark is an alteration of a licensed work, which a share-alike or attribution licence does not permit us to make silently. So the file is refused instead. Commons flags these with {{watermark}} in the wikitext, which is how the survey caught it, but the flag is applied by volunteers and its absence proves nothing — look at the pixels. tools/atlas-licence.mjs already declared this blind spot in its own header ("it reads TEXT; a file whose page is correct and whose pixels carry a watermark passes"). The limit was known and unenforced; this is the rule that enforces it, at survey time, by eye.

  • "No known copyright restrictions" is not a licence, and the National Library of Ireland's Flickr stream is where that trap lives. Recorded 25 July 2026 because the place survey hit it four times in one place: the finest period images of Dalkey — Robert French, Lawrence Collection, exactly the class clause 7 clears — carry {{Flickr-no known copyright restrictions}} and no public-domain tag of any kind, sourced from nli.ie's stream. Both grounds already appear above; what is new is how good the rejected images are. The temptation is real and it should be expected rather than resisted by memory.

  • Cleared: period photographs attributed to Robert French (d. 1917; public domain in Ireland and the EU since 1988); contemporary photographs under CC0, CC BY or CC BY-SA, with Irish freedom of panorama (CRRA 2000 s.93) clearing the architecture; Thom's 1904 directory; the 1922 text.

  • Not cleared, and dropped: any page scan of a 16 June 1904 Dublin newspaper — no free source exists, and the digitisers hold the scans even though the 1904 text is out of copyright; Joyce's handwriting and any manuscript image — the Rosenbach expressly neither grants nor denies permission, and unpublished Joyce remains in copyright in the UK until 2039; photographic portraits of Joyce (Abbott and Freund both have active licensors); the separate tram map.

  • The map layer is settled, and the licence was read rather than inferred (19 July 2026, catch C-33). The Ordnance Survey six-inch Ireland sheets come from the National Library of Scotland, whose per-image tag on the Dublin sheets reads Re-use: CC-BY (NLS) and whose terms page resolves that to CC BY 4.0 — not NC, not ND, not SA. The required credit, verbatim: "Reproduced with the permission of the National Library of Scotland", plus a link to maps.nls.uk for online use. County Dublin is held in a revision surveyed 1906–1909 and printed 1909–1912, which is the layer this project wants. Two doors that look right and are not: the Digital Repository of Ireland applies its own CC BY-NC-ND reuse licence, which fails this bar, and links back to NLS anyway; and Wikimedia Commons holds no OS Ireland Dublin sheets at all. Go to NLS directly. Build constraint: the NLS IIIF server caps a request at roughly half a megapixel, so there is no whole-sheet download — the Atlas must tile at 512×512 or smaller. Corrected 25 July 2026, by testing it instead of repeating it (L-31). The conclusion holds and the number was wrong by nearly half. The server declares "maxArea": 270000 in its own info.json and enforces it to the pixel: 600×450 (270,000) returns 200, and 601×450 (270,450) returns 403. So the cap is about 0.27 megapixels, not 0.5. A 512×512 tile is 262,144 pixels — 97.1% of the cap, not half of it. The tiling conclusion survives by three per cent rather than by a factor of two, and a session that had trusted "roughly half a megapixel" would have sized its tiles at around 700×700 and collected 403s. info.json also declares tiles: [{width: 512, height: 512, …}], so 512 is the server's own tile size and departing from it buys at most 34 requests out of 759 while losing the tile-aligned cache. One sheet is 33 × 23 = 759 tiles, roughly 14 MB and about nine minutes at a polite request rate. The whole-sheet claim is confirmed: the only pre-rendered sizes offered are three thumbnails, the largest 521 px wide.

  • The NLS front door is shut to any tool, and that has a consequence for this clause. maps.nls.uk sits behind an AWS WAF: every non-browser request returns HTTP 405 "Human Verification", and a real browser gets a security-check interstitial. So the per-image Re-use: CC-BY (NLS) tag recorded above could not be re-read on 25 July 2026. It was read once, on 19 July 2026, by the survey that produced catch C-33; nothing since has confirmed it, the Wayback Machine holds no capture of any Irish six-inch sheet's view page, and the archived copyright page of 3 December 2025 agrees with what is written here but is not the per-image tag. Stated as a limit rather than left implied (L-18): this clause's licence line is currently unverifiable by tool, and someone should open https://maps.nls.uk/view/247667874 by hand and read the box. Nothing is being sourced from NLS until that is done.

It reads Re-use: CC-BY (NLS). — the tag recorded above, unchanged, with CC-BY rendered as a hyperlink and NLS's own "Order this map" bar below it. Catch C-63's freeze is lifted, and it is lifted by a record rather than by this sentence: the reading lives in data/atlas-document-survey.json under osSheets.licenceReadings, every document slot's outstanding list derives from it, and §11w fails the build in both directions — if a reading is current and any slot still announces the freeze, or if a slot announces a reading no record supports. Prose thaws when somebody remembers to edit it in eighteen places, which is catch C-42; a record thaws by existing.

    Three limits travel with that reading and are part of it (A5, L-17). (1) The evidence is a screenshot crop carrying no URL bar and no sheet number, so it does not itself establish which sheet — the author was asked to open Sheet 18 at /view/247667874 and his sending the box back is his report that he read it there; that report is the source, and it is marked as his report. (2) CC-BY is a link and the link target is not in the crop, so the reading confirms the tag and not that it still resolves to 4.0 rather than another version; the 4.0 above rests on the 19 July reading and the archived copyright page, which agree. (3) The tag is per image and the Atlas would draw on ten sheets — one reading clears one sheet, and no reading is asserted for the other nine.

    A tool still cannot read it. The WAF is unchanged: re-tested 26 July 2026 from a real browser engine against both maps.nls.uk and map-view.nls.uk, and both serve the human-verification interstitial rather than the page. That is why the ruling named a person.

    And the map layer is still gated, on something other than its licence. The institutional-marks question (catch C-72, learning L-34) is unruled and reaches every institutional scan. An Ordnance Survey sheet photographed by a library is an institutional scan, and if that question goes the literal way these sheets fail on their marks whatever this clause says about their licence. The acquisition route itself does not need the front door, and is recorded in data/atlas-document-survey.json: geoserver.nls.uk's WFS sheet index → the sheet's IMAGE id → map-view.nls.uk's IIIF endpoint. Both are ungated. A public S3 XYZ tileset of the same edition also exists at mapseries-tilesets.s3.amazonaws.com/os/dublin_2nd/{z}/{x}/{y}.png. Neither route changes the licence question; they only mean the WAF is not the obstacle.

  • The object layer is acquired: 17 of 17 cleared slots hold a file. Episodes 4, 7, 8, 10, 13, 15 and 16 were cleared on 19 July and never fetched; they were fetched on 25 July, and every one of the seventeen now carries the clearance-comparison's own verdict in artifacts/atlas-of-one-day/provenance.json — the licence Commons reports today against the licence the rights survey recorded, agreeing on family and, where both state one, on version. Before that run the gate had never fired on a real image. Clearance and acquisition are two different facts and this note keeps them apart.

  • Where a photograph would mislead, use a document instead. 7 Eccles Street was demolished in 1967. In a caption-free atlas a convincing photograph of the surviving terrace would read as Bloom's door and be the wrong house, and there would be no caption to say so. The Thom's 1904 entry for the address is the honest element, and the better one.

Practical rule for the build: anything on the site that is not Joyce's 1922 text or our own writing carries an attribution line. If it can't be attributed, it doesn't go up.
